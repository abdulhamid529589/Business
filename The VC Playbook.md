## 1. How K Capital Was Born — The Origin Story

### Sasha's Unusual Entry into Business

Sasha Mirchandani grew up in Malabar Hill, Mumbai — one of the wealthiest addresses in India. His father founded Onida, a once-dominant Indian electronics brand. But rather than an entitled handover, his father's philosophy was clear:

> *"No entitlement. You must do it yourself."*

**Day 1 at Onida:** Sasha was told to take the train — second class. Having lived abroad, this was a shock. The experience of getting crushed in the crowd, selling TVs shop-to-shop in difficult areas, and being posted to Mangalore for branch experience taught him one thing:

> *"Appreciate what people have to go through at the bottom to get to the top."*

### The Pivot to Venture Capital

Around 1999–2000, two observations converged in Sasha's mind:

1. **Industry insight:** He attended a YPO talk where a famous industrialist described moving from textiles to pharmaceuticals — better margins, better moats. Electronics (the Onida business) was brutally competitive with thin margins.

2. **Access to capital gap:** His father had struggled to raise capital for every business he built in the late 1970s and 80s. Fast forward 25 years — talented entrepreneurs were still struggling to find capital despite the world having far more of it.

> *"It's not fair. Why can't that smart entrepreneur get capital? I'm not some Gandhi, but I felt that's not right."*

The VC industry barely existed in India in 1999–2000. Sasha's insight: *Can we help entrepreneurs AND get into a new industry simultaneously?*

### The First Investment — Fractal Analytics (Voodoo.com)

A friend introduced him to a team building a product comparison website. They hadn't even come for funding — they wanted Onida TVs to compare on their site. But Sasha liked the founders immediately.

A few months later, they came for capital. Sasha invested from the Onida balance sheet. Within 3 months, the board questioned why a TV company was investing in software. Sasha bought the shares out personally.

The company pivoted — from a comparison website → data mining → AI analytics.

> *"I told them: you're rockstars. Fold this up and come up with any new idea within IT. Three months later, they came with a new plan. That became Fractal Analytics — today one of the largest AI companies out of India."*

**Lesson:** The founders mattered far more than the original idea.

## 2. The One Thing That Predicts Billionaire Founders

### The 10 Entrepreneurs Exercise

If you put 10 entrepreneurs in a room — all of them look great, all raised seed funds, all seem exceptional — one will become a billionaire in the next decade. What separates the one from the nine?

> *"The one is doing it for the right reasons."*

### What Are the "Right Reasons"?

The best company origin story is one of these:
1. The founder personally experienced the pain point they are solving
2. Someone very close to the founder (parent, sibling, friend) experienced it

> *"These founders have a clear purpose. They say: this pain was suffered by my father or by me. I need to solve this. I am on a mission."*

**Sasha's data:** Founders building around a genuine personal pain point **outperform opportunistic founders 9-to-1.**

### What Are the "Wrong Reasons"?

Starting a company because:
- It's glamorous / sexy
- Everyone around you is doing it
- You see wealth creation as the shortcut goal

Sasha doesn't say this is morally wrong. He says:

> *"Entrepreneurship and making it successful — the probability of success is already remote. The first signs of trouble, the glamour disappears."*

If you started only for glamour, you quit when the reality hits. If you started for purpose, you push through.

### Can You Start for Money?

Yes — and some people doing it for money have done fine. But:
- Sasha's diligence process tries to understand *why* deeply
- Founders who can't articulate a clear pain point get far more scrutiny
- The truly exceptional ones — the Jagtesh Munjals, the Rajan Kitas of the world — ended up making so much money they "don't know what to do with it." Purpose came first, wealth followed.

## 3. How to Identify the Right Founder

### The Hell or High Water Test

The primary personality trait Sasha screens for:

> *"Are they entrepreneurs that are hell or high water — no matter what, they will figure it out. No excuse."*

**Examples:**
- **Deep Kalra (MakeMyTrip):** Went from hundreds of employees to 20–30 during the dot-com crash. Rebuilt. Refused to die.
- **Ashish Kashyap (ibibo):** Crashed to 6 people from 300. IPO'd at 40% up. This week in the news.
- **Shiprocket founders:** Down to ₹1 crore in the bank. Told the team: "We will not die."

### The Conscientious Person Test

> *"The number one trait of success is: are you a conscientious person? You say what you'll do. You do it. Talk is cheap."*

**How to identify conscientiousness in diligence:**
- Ask for examples where everyone/everything said they should give up — but they didn't
- Look for specific stories, not general claims
- The specificity and emotion of the story reveals whether it's real

### The Portfolio of Questions

During founder evaluation, K Capital asks across multiple dimensions:
- Examples of refusing to quit
- Collaborative vs. political personality
- Can they attract great people?
- Would the people who know them work *for* them again?
- What is their relationship with integrity?

## 4. The Founding Team — Who You Need and Why

### The Two Non-Negotiables at Day Zero

**1. A Builder**
Someone who can build a world-class product. A mediocre product can limp along early but eventually collapses without quality at the core.

**2. A Seller**
> *"Entrepreneurship is selling. When you start, what are you selling? Nothing. There is no business."*

The seller is selling:
- The vision to early customers
- The company to investors like Sasha
- The opportunity to early team members who leave safe jobs

**The rare combination:** Someone who is both builder and seller. Sasha's example: Srikanth Velamakanni of Fractal Analytics — can code at genius level and sell to anyone. This is why great companies get built; this combination is extraordinarily rare.

### The Dreamer-Developer Duality

Another framing Sasha uses:

```
Dreamer: Thinks big. Sees 100x outcomes. Keeps the vision alive.
Developer: Builds the thing. Turns the dream into real product.
```

VCs need to see both because VC math requires 50x–100x outcomes, not base hits.

> *"We're not in the business of bunts. We're in the home run business."*

### Complementarity Over Duplication

Two people with identical skills is a wasted slot. The best founding teams are intentional about complementarity:

- If you are great at sales, find a great engineer
- If you are a great product person, find someone who can sell
- The third co-founder fills the remaining operational gap

**Single founder exception:** Sasha used to avoid single founders. He changed this belief. Examples: Vijay Shekhar Sharma (Paytm), Foxtail (K Capital portfolio). The key: can the single founder attract exceptional co-founders or early team members through sheer vision and energy?

## 5. Founder-Market Fit — The Most Underrated Filter

### The Concept

Even a great founder in the wrong market is a bad investment. K Capital now asks: **Why is THIS founding team the right team for THIS market?**

### What Founder-Market Fit Looks Like

**Strong fit:**
- Former industry insider who identified a gap their company wouldn't solve
- Personal pain point in the exact category
- Deep domain expertise that gives them an unfair insight

**Weak fit:**
- Amazing resume, great personality — but has to operate in the field, not the spreadsheet
- Example: An alcohol brand founder who is brilliant on paper but won't do the distribution grind, the customs management, the 3 AM supply chain calls

> *"The minute the hard work starts — going to the market, managing customs officers — the wrong founder folds. The right founder says: this is my DNA. I'll do it."*

### Can Outsiders Win?

Yes — the naivety argument:

> *"Because they don't realize how tough it is, they jump in. And then figure it out."*

Sasha's example: Bhavish Aggarwal (Ola Electric). If he had known how hard building a gigafactory is, he might not have started. He didn't know — so he started, and figured it out.

**The key:** Outsiders need compensating traits — exceptional determination, the hell or high water quality — to compensate for the lack of domain knowledge.

### The Spiky Founder

For outsider founders, Sasha looks for what he calls "spike" — something that shows the founder went unusually deep despite not having direct experience:

- They spent months with customers before building anything
- They identified a non-obvious insight that insiders missed
- Their knowledge of the problem is already at expert level through research and obsession

> *"The insight should be so unique that the founder is educating me. I should have no clue about it. If I'm learning something genuinely new in that meeting, that's a great sign."*

## 6. The Perfect Pitch Deck — 10 Slides Formula

### The Core Problem with Most Pitches

> *"If I had to see 100 pitches, 90 will be cluttered. The more excitement the entrepreneur has, the more data they put in. But what I want is clarity of thought and simplicity."*

### The 10-Slide Formula

| Slide # | Content | Key Guidance |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Team** | Sasha wants to see who he's meeting first. Who are these people? |
| **2** | **Problem** | One clear line. What pain exists? |
| **3** | **Solution** | One clear line. What are you doing about it? |
| **4** | **Why Now?** | Critical. Why does this problem need solving *today*? Tailwinds, regulatory change, timing. |
| **5** | **Product / Demo** | One slide or a short video showing how it works. Simple. |
| **6** | **Traction** | If you have a few months of data, show it. Revenue growing? Retention numbers? |
| **7** | **Competition** | Where do you stand? Be honest. |
| **8** | **TAM** | Not "1% of $50B." Show the small, fast-growing market you're going after and why it will explode. |
| **9** | **Business Model** | How do you make money? |
| **10** | **The Ask** | How much are you raising? What will you use it for? |

### The TAM Slide — What Not to Do

> *"Don't show me the '1% of the market' slides. Those are the worst. That's the laziest slide ever. Delete the presentation."*

**What to show instead:**
- A small, new, growing TAM — because in cluttered large markets, you can't differentiate
- Evidence that the TAM is growing fast (or will grow based on a trend)
- OR: Your unique insight as to why a new market is emerging

Sasha is comfortable with small TAMs if the growth vector is clear. A fast-growing small market is better than a slow-growing large one.

### The Dream-Building Element

By the end of the pitch, the founder must have taken the investor on a dream journey:

> *"If you can't take me on your dream in that one hour — I have to suddenly feel enthralled, like 'wow, this is amazing' — then you've failed."*

Show: If everything goes right, how big can this become? Make the investor co-dream with you.

## 7. Why Failed Founders Are Valuable

### Sasha's Counterintuitive View

> *"We love to invest in failed founders."*

### Why?

A founder who has failed once has:
- Real scar tissue from execution reality
- A clearer understanding of what went wrong
- Usually removed the factors that caused the failure
- The grit to keep going despite the stigma

**Average number of companies a founder must fail before the big one:** 5–6

> *"Entrepreneurship is not an overnight success. The data tells us that on average, you have to fail five or six companies before the sixth one becomes really large."*

### What Makes a Good Answer About Past Failure

Sasha listens for:
1. **Precision** — Can they explain exactly what went wrong?
2. **Honesty** — No excuses, no blame-shifting
3. **Learning application** — What specifically is different this time?
4. **Timing argument** — Was it just the wrong time? Is the timing right now?

**Red flags in failure answers:**
- Blaming the market, the economy, the investors, the team (not themselves)
- Vague explanations ("it just didn't work out")
- No change in approach for the new venture

### India's Failure Stigma Problem

India still carries significant social stigma around business failure. Unlike Silicon Valley, where failure is often worn as a badge, Indian founders often try to hide or minimize past failures.

> *"In India, the job is the most important thing. If you fail again, it's shameful. People I know who've been exceptionally good — they go back to a job because they feel embarrassed."*

Sasha's recommendation: Successful founders must openly talk about their failures. Role models matter.

## 8. Due Diligence — How to Tell a Storyteller from an Executor

### The Problem

Some founders are exceptional storytellers who can make you dream big in the room. They charm investors, close capital, and then underdeliver on execution. How do you catch this?

### The Diligence Framework

**Step 1: Reference Checks — 10 to 20 people per founder**

Who to speak to:
- Former colleagues at previous jobs
- University professors (if early career)
- Co-workers who reported to or worked alongside the founder
- Anyone who has seen them in high-pressure situations

**Key questions asked:**
1. Give me an example where this person refused to give up despite all signals saying they should.
2. Would you fund this person? Rate them 1–10 and explain why.
3. Why are they NOT putting their own money into this?
4. Were they collaborative or political?
5. Could they attract great people to work for them?
6. **Would YOU leave your current job to work for them?** (The answer reveals true conviction about the founder.)

**Step 2: Integrity Check**

> *"If there is even a doubt about integrity, we will drop the deal right there."*

How to check integrity:
- Ask reference checks directly: *"Are there any integrity issues I should know about?"*
- People will often signal discomfort without stating it directly — read the hesitation
- Ask: *"Have there been situations where they bent the rules?"*

**Red flag behaviors during the integrity check:**
- Reference checks giving vague answers instead of enthusiastic endorsements
- Evidence of blaming others for failures
- Pattern of job-hopping without clear narrative
- Spending company money lavishly at early stages

**Step 3: Founder-Market Fit Validation**

Ask the reference check contact: *"Is this the right founding team for this market? If not, why?"*

Sometimes the answer reveals a fatal flaw: a founder who looks perfect on paper is actually mismatched to what the business requires operationally.

### What Turns Sasha Off Instantly

| Signal | Why It's a Red Flag |
|---|---|
| Very political behavior | Will undermine team trust and company culture |
| Jumping from job to job without clear reason | Pattern of quitting when it gets hard |
| Blaming everyone else for failures | Can't self-assess; won't fix problems |
| Spending investor money on luxury (business class, expensive offices) | Wrong priorities; treats company money as personal |
| Excessive salary at seed stage | Equity should be the motivation, not cash |

## 9. Valuation — The Art of Pricing a Company That Has Nothing

### The Honest Answer

> *"It's more art than science."*

At the seed stage, there is:
- No balance sheet
- No P&L
- Often no product
- Just 3–4 founders and an idea

So valuation comes from:

**1. How convincingly the founder articulates their vision**
Great founders argue their valuation compellingly and negotiate well.

**2. Market comparables**
Roughly what companies in this category and stage are being valued at in the market.

**3. How much K Capital wants the deal**
If they love the founders, they may overpay. But:

> *"We have overpaid many times. Our goal is for the business to become $1B, $2B, $10B. At that scale, whether we paid 20% more at entry doesn't matter."*

**4. Ownership math**
The one constraint: K Capital cannot go below a certain ownership percentage or the investment won't move the needle for the fund. If a founder's valuation would leave K with 1–2% ownership, it's a structural problem regardless of quality.

### Typical Seed Valuation Range

₹5 crore to ₹20 crore enterprise value at seed stage (roughly $600K to $2.4M USD). Money raised at seed: ₹8 crore to ₹16 crore ($1–2M).

## 10. Fund Size — Why Small Funds Outperform Large Funds

### Sasha's Counterintuitive Thesis

Most VCs chase large fund sizes for management fees and prestige. K Capital has deliberately kept all funds **under $100 million** (sub-₹850 crore).

> *"In India, for early stage venture capital, sub-$100 million is the sweet spot. After a certain number, performance starts declining."*

### The Math Behind Small Fund Superiority

**Scenario: $400M fund**

Suppose they find 2–3 great companies:
- Company A: 6% ownership → reaches $2B → $120M return
- Company B: 7% ownership → reaches $3B → $210M return
- Total from top companies: ~$330M

But the fund size is $400M, so top performers barely return 0.8x the fund. You need the average companies to do well too — which statistically they won't.

**Scenario: $100M fund**

Same 2–3 companies:
- Company A: 10% ownership → reaches $2B → $200M return (2x the fund by itself)
- Company B: 10% → $3B → $300M (3x the fund)

Even one great company returns the entire fund. That's the model K Capital runs.

```
Fund Size Rule:
Small fund + High ownership % in winners = Exceptional returns
Large fund + Lower ownership % in winners = Mediocre returns
```

### The 30-Company Portfolio Strategy

K Capital targets 30 companies per fund. Every single one is evaluated as if it could become the next Porter or Fractal — a fund-returner.

> *"If we don't think every company can be multi-billion, we're screwed because the odds of finding one are already so low. We can't say '5x is fine for these 10.' No. Every entrepreneur has to wow us."*

## 11. The Home Run Model — How VC Math Works

### The Power Law Reality

In any VC fund:
- 40% of companies → limited return despite best efforts
- ~50% → small exits, okay outcomes
- 10% → home runs that return the fund

> *"Winners have to return the entire fund bare minimum. Ideally 50% of the fund from a single company."*

**K Capital's Fund 1 proof:**
- Porter → expected to return the fund multiple times over
- Healthkart → expected to return the fund at least twice
- Network (InMobi affiliate) → massive return

These three alone justify the entire fund. Everything else is bonus.

### What This Means for Founders

> *"Any company you pitch to us — every single one — has to have the potential to be the next Fractal, the next Porter, the next Network. If the maximum it can reach is ₹50 crore, we won't fund it."*

A ₹50 crore outcome is great for an angel investor doing 5x returns. But for a VC fund doing math on fund-level returns, it doesn't move the needle.

### The Missed Companies

Sasha is remarkably honest about what they missed:

- **Zomato** — came and pitched
- **Grofers / Blinkit** — came and pitched
- **Meesho** — came and pitched
- **OLA** — came and pitched
- **Zepto** — Adit came at 19 years old. They loved the founder but not the Kirana-kart idea

> *"If you're not missing iconic companies, you're not in the mix. You're not even being considered for the great deals."*

The lesson: the very best investors still miss great companies. What matters is that you're in the conversation for them.

## 12. Founder Salary — What to Pay Yourself at Each Stage

### The Core Philosophy

> *"You can be rich or you can be wealthy. As a founder, the goal is wealthy — which comes from equity, not salary."*

The salary should be enough to sustain basic life — but should keep you uncomfortable enough that you remain hungry.

> *"Your salary should be pinching you. But you shouldn't be thinking about it too much. It should be nowhere near your market price."*

### Salary Guidelines by Stage

| Stage | Valuation | Fundraise | Under 30 Founder | Over 40 Founder |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Seed** | ₹5–20 crore | ₹8–16 crore | ₹25–40 lakh/year | ₹50–60 lakh/year |
| **Series A** | ~₹40–80 crore | ₹40–60 crore | +10% over seed | +10% over seed |
| **Series B** | ₹150 crore+ | ₹100–150 crore | ₹50–60 lakh/year | ₹75–80 lakh/year |
| **Pre-IPO** | ₹1,000 crore+ | Large | Up to ₹1–2 crore | Up to ₹1–2 crore |

*All figures approximate; actual ranges depend on city, sector, and fund preference.*

### When Can a Founder Take Secondary?

**Secondary** = Selling some of your personal shares to an investor to put cash in your pocket.

- **Series B:** Too early. You're selling ownership of the company too soon.
- **Pre-IPO / Late Growth Stage:** Appropriate. You've proven the business. A modest secondary lets you buy a house, reduce personal financial stress.
- **Trigger:** When personal financial pressure is genuinely distracting you from building.

> *"For 8–10–12 years, we want that founder to be so obsessed about their company that 90% of their net worth is in it. That alignment is very exciting for us."*

## 13. How to Attract Capital — The Four Things That Work

### Recap from the Episode

The four things that attract investor capital (in Sasha's order):

```
1. Great Guy / Founder Quality (most important)
2. Deep Market (large profit pool OR fast-growing small pool)
3. Cheap / Good Value (price feels like a bargain)
4. Personal Business Model (investor sees their own story in your pitch)
```

### Making Your Business Model Feel Personal to an Investor

> *"If I can make my ideas seem personal to the person I'm sitting across from, he'll likely give."*

**How to do this:**
- Listen before you talk. Understand what the investor cares about personally.
- Observe: What has their fund invested in? What does their background suggest they understand?
- Customize your framing to connect with their worldview
- Don't present an agenda — converse and build on what they say

The 99-1 Rule:
- 90% listening to the investor / prospect
- 9% reframing their problem back to them with more precision
- 1% giving a pinpointed solution

> *"In meetings where I've done 100% selling vs. meetings where I've done 90% listening and reframed their problem — the conversion difference is enormous."*

### The Referenced Introduction

Sasha explicitly says: come to him through a relevant, trusted reference. Cold emails from unknown senders are filtered through thousands of deals/year.

> *"If you can find a way to reach me through a good reference — that's half the battle."*

The ability to navigate your way to the right person IS a test of the founder's hustle and resourcefulness.

## 14. Three Metrics Every Founder Must Track

*(Covered in detail in the Ashish Mohapatra notes — Sasha echoes these principles)*

Sasha's aligned view:

**1. Revenue growing consistently** (3–6 months) → Product-Market Fit
**2. Margins above cost of capital** → Profitable PMF
**3. Free cash flow sufficient to self-fund next phase** → Scalable PMF

Additional metrics Sasha values:
- **Retention numbers** — especially for consumer apps; "show us retention, not just acquisition"
- **NPS** — a proxy for product love and word-of-mouth potential
- **Burn multiple** — how much are you spending for every rupee of new ARR?

## 15. Sector Allocation — Where to Put Money Now

### Sasha's Poker Chip Exercise

Given ₹100 crore to allocate across five sectors:

| Sector | Allocation | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| **AI & Technology** | ₹50 crore (50%) | *"The largest TAM I've ever seen in my life. Every company in the world will need an AI strategy."* Bubble risk exists, but this category is effectively infinite. |
| **Health Tech** | ₹20 crore (20%) | India is massively under-penetrated in health — hospital beds, digital health, mental health. One of the hardest sectors to crack, which is exactly why Sasha loves it. |
| **EV & Mobility** | ₹10 crore (10%) | India approaching 5% EV penetration — the inflection point where China exploded. May increase allocation as this tips. Examples: Ather Energy. |
| **Fintech & Digital Payments** | ₹15 crore (15%) | Legacy banks being disrupted. Multiple multi-billion outcomes already (Razorpay, MSwipes, Snapmint). Still large whitespace. |
| **Renewable Energy** | ₹5 crore (5%) | Massive need but PE/conglomerate game, not seed VC. K Capital's small check won't move the needle. |

### Why AI Gets Half the Money

> *"There will be no company left that can survive without an AI strategy. Right now there's negativity — 'companies aren't really using it.' I agree. But when it comes — and it will come — if you're not ready, god forbid."*

The consumer AI sub-thesis that K Capital is particularly excited about: AI products for small towns and Tier 2/3 India — a market that global AI labs (OpenAI, Anthropic) have zero interest in serving.

**Example portfolio company: Super Living**
- AI-native platform for small-town India
- Helps users navigate government schemes, local issues
- Built by ex-Meesho founders who understand this audience
- Already received $7M in growth capital; "literally exploding"

### The One Sector That Doesn't Fit VC

**Deep Tech / Defense / Space:** K Capital is interested but constrained. The problem: deep tech companies need $100M+ in capital before generating revenue. A $100M fund simply can't allocate enough at the seed stage to matter at the later stages. However, they do fund **ancillary businesses** that sell into defense, space, and drone companies.

## 16. Arrogant Founders vs. Loved Founders — Do Nice Guys Win?

### The Uncomfortable Data

Sasha is honest: some of the best-returning founders were not the nicest people.

**The arrogant/difficult list:**
- Travis Kalanick (Uber) — documented behavior issues; built an iconic company
- Steve Jobs — fired by his own company; came back; built the most valuable company in history
- Larry Ellison — fired entire floors of engineers; shipped anyway
- Elon Musk — widely criticized; keeps building category-defining companies

**The loved/gentle list:**
- Larry Page and Sergey Brin (Google) — universally respected
- Pran and Utam (Porter) — quiet, calm, deeply determined
- Ankit Nagori (Cure.fit) — "sweetest founder in India" but fiercely competitive

### Sasha's Conclusion

> *"You cannot stereotype. Arrogant founders sometimes win. Loved founders sometimes win. The common thread is competitive drive, not personality type."*

**What matters more than personality:**
- Internal drive to win
- Clarity of mission
- Execution discipline

### The Steve Jobs Nuance

Steve Jobs wasn't born arrogant. As his career evolved and he was treated poorly (fired from his own company), he became more take-no-prisoners. But even he:
- Treated close collaborators (Jony Ive, Tim Cook) with deep respect
- Built a succession plan that kept Apple thriving after his death
- Was selective about who he was brutal toward vs. who he protected

> *"The people who resonated with him, he treated them very well. He built an amazing collaborative team."*

## 17. The Delusion Trap — When Self-Belief Becomes Self-Harm

### The Fine Line

Delusional belief is good at the start — you need it to believe in something the world doesn't believe in yet. But at some point, it can become a liability.

### Signs That a Founder Has Crossed the Line

| Healthy Delusion | Toxic Delusion |
|---|---|
| "I refuse to give up; I'll figure it out" | "Everyone is against me; I am the greatest and they don't see it" |
| "We missed our milestone; let's diagnose why" | "It's the market's fault, the investors' fault, the team's fault" |
| "The business is struggling; let me adapt" | "The business is rolling along fine" (when it's clearly not) |
| Cuts costs when capital is running low | Keeps burning, convinced a funding round is around the corner |

### The Opportunity Cost Reality

For founders whose businesses are "rolling along" but not breaking out, Sasha will actively nudge them toward stopping:

> *"Leave this and have another shot at gold. Refresh your brain. The opportunity cost of staying is too high."*

A determined entrepreneur who has spent 5 years on a stagnant business might be far better served starting fresh — with more knowledge, better network, clearer thesis.

### What K Capital Does When They Write Off a Company

1. Formally tell their LPs the residual stake is worth zero
2. Tell the founder: *"We respect your decision to keep running this. But we won't proactively follow up."*
3. Remain available if the founder calls with a genuine emergency
4. Never hold a grudge — will still consider funding the founder again

> *"We will fund second, third, fourth-time founders. No grudges."*

## 18. Corporate Culture — What Actually Works at Scale

### Sasha's Personal Culture Test

At Onida, he saw firsthand that company culture is absorbed through experience, not taught through memos.

> *"Culture is something I've seen in action and seen the benefit of elsewhere — where I was a part of that culture, not the one setting it up. Those are the ones I've invited into my own work."*

### Three Requirements for Real Culture

1. **You must have experienced it yourself** — not read about it
2. **You must do it yourself** — people copy what they see, not what they're told
3. **Keep it simple** — If you can't define your culture in half a sentence, it won't stick

### What Kills Culture

Trying to engineer culture consciously for the sake of it. If you need a consultant to define your values, they won't be real values.

> *"Culture is something that, if you start thinking about doing it consciously to make people change their behavior — it won't happen."*

### The Best Culture Metric

Not engagement scores. Not "best place to work" rankings.

The real metric: **Is the operating business growing?**

> *"I've seen companies where feedback ratings are so high, everything is great, best place to work — but the operating business is doing badly. What's the point? As a capitalist, I'll pick the founder whose business is winning."*

## 19. Key Frameworks Summary

### The Founder Quality Stack

```
Level 1: Hell or High Water (will not quit under any circumstance)
Level 2: Conscientious (does what they say they will do)
Level 3: Complimentary Team (builder + seller + third skill)
Level 4: Founder-Market Fit (right person for THIS business)
Level 5: Clear Pain Point (personal or deeply researched)
Level 6: Unique Insight (teaches the VC something new)
```

### The Pitch Deck Formula

```
Slide 1: Team
Slide 2: Problem (1 line)
Slide 3: Solution (1 line)
Slide 4: Why Now? (timing, tailwinds)
Slide 5: Product / Demo
Slide 6: Traction
Slide 7: Competition
Slide 8: TAM (small + growing, not 1% of $50B)
Slide 9: Business Model
Slide 10: The Ask
```

### VC Fund Math

```
Fund Rule: Winners must return 50–100% of the fund minimum.

Small Fund ($100M):
  10% ownership × $2B exit = $200M = 2x the fund ✓

Large Fund ($400M):
  6% ownership × $2B exit = $120M = 0.3x the fund ✗
  (Need many more large exits to return the fund)

Conclusion: Smaller fund + higher ownership = better VC math
```

### Sector Allocation (2025–2026)

```
AI & Technology: 50% — largest TAM ever; every company needs it
Health Tech: 20% — massively underpenetrated in India
Fintech: 15% — legacy disruption still ongoing
EV & Mobility: 10% — approaching inflection point
Renewable Energy: 5% — real opportunity but PE, not VC game
```

### The Four Things That Attract Capital

```
1. Great Founder (most important — spotter + resource builder + people attractor)
2. Deep Market (large or fast-growing profit pool)
3. Cheap (price is below perceived value)
4. Personal Business Model (investor sees their own story in your pitch)
```

## 20. Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Hell or High Water Founder** | Sasha's phrase for a founder who refuses to quit no matter what circumstances arise |
| **Conscientious** | The single most important personality trait: doing exactly what you say you will do |
| **Founder-Market Fit** | The degree to which a founding team is the natural, right team to solve a specific market's problem |
| **Pain Point Founder** | A founder who personally experienced or deeply witnessed the problem they are solving |
| **Opportunistic Founder** | A founder who starts a company because it's trendy or lucrative, without deep personal connection to the problem |
| **Spiky Founder** | An outsider founder who compensates for lack of direct domain experience with unusual depth of research and insight |
| **Home Run** | A single investment that returns 50–100%+ of the entire fund |
| **Bunt** | A small, safe investment return — what Sasha deliberately does NOT optimize for |
| **Recycle Clause** | A fund provision allowing early exit proceeds to be reinvested in new companies within the same fund, increasing the number of bets |
| **Seed Stage** | Pre-product or very early product stage; typically $1–2M raised at $5–20M valuation |
| **Series A** | First significant institutional round; typically $5M raised; business has initial traction |
| **Series B** | Growth round; typically $15M+; business model is proven, scaling begins |
| **Secondary** | A founder or early employee selling some of their personal shares to an incoming investor — putting cash in their pocket while diluting themselves |
| **TAM (Total Addressable Market)** | The total revenue opportunity for a product if it captured 100% of its market |
| **Fund Returner** | A single investment outcome that returns the entire capital committed to a fund |
| **LP (Limited Partner)** | The investors who put money into a VC fund (e.g., family offices, endowments, HNIs) |
| **Power Law** | The mathematical reality that a tiny % of investments produce the vast majority of VC returns |
| **Consumer AI** | AI products built for everyday consumer use, especially in Tier 2/3 markets — K Capital's emerging thesis |
| **Edge Case / Head Scratcher** | Sasha's term for investments in new categories that make people say "why would that work?" — where the biggest opportunities often hide |
| **ESOP Liquidity** | Converting employee stock options into actual cash, not just paper value |
| **NPS (Net Promoter Score)** | Customer satisfaction metric; 40+ is considered "great" |

## 21. Self-Assessment Checklist

### For Founders Before Pitching

**On your motivation:**
- [ ] Can I articulate in one compelling sentence *why* I personally am the right person to solve this problem?
- [ ] Do I have a genuine pain point story — personal or someone close to me?
- [ ] Is my reason for starting this company something I can explain at 3 AM after 3 years of struggle?

**On your team:**
- [ ] Do we have a builder AND a seller?
- [ ] Is our team complementary or duplicative in skills?
- [ ] Can we each attract exceptional people to join us?

**On your pitch:**
- [ ] Is my pitch deck 10 slides or fewer?
- [ ] Can I explain the problem in one sentence?
- [ ] Can I explain the solution in one sentence?
- [ ] Do I have a "Why Now" slide with concrete tailwinds?
- [ ] Have I removed the "1% of $50B market" slide?
- [ ] Does my pitch end with the investor dreaming bigger about the market I showed them?

**On your insight:**
- [ ] Is there something in my pitch that a seasoned investor with expert analysts does not already know?
- [ ] Can I educate a smart investor about my space in the first 10–15 minutes of a meeting?

**On founder-market fit:**
- [ ] Can I answer convincingly: "Why are you specifically the right person to build this?"
- [ ] Have I spent enough time with customers to know their pain better than anyone else in the room?

### For Investors / Aspiring Angels

**On evaluating founders:**
- [ ] Have I asked for specific examples of when this person refused to give up?
- [ ] Have I done 10–20 reference checks including at least 5 people who've worked with them?
- [ ] Have I asked references: "Would YOU leave your job to work for this person?"
- [ ] Is there any doubt about integrity? If yes — have I dropped the deal?
- [ ] Is this the right founding team for THIS market specifically?

**On fund math:**
- [ ] Does every investment I make need to have the potential to return my portfolio?
- [ ] Am I being disciplined about ownership percentage at entry?
- [ ] Is my fund size calibrated to the return math of the companies I'm targeting?

**On sector thesis:**
- [ ] Do I have a thesis about where AI will create new consumer categories in India?
- [ ] Am I looking at health tech given India's massive under-penetration?
- [ ] Am I avoiding sectors where the opportunity is real but the minimum viable capital is out of reach for my fund size?
